Part One · The Centerpiece

How we make it

Six steps, in order. This is the whole process, and the reason it can be described in six steps is that there is nothing hidden in it.

1

Fresh frozen

Harvested at peak ripeness and frozen immediately, before the aroma has a chance to leave.

The detail

Terpenes are volatile, meaning they evaporate readily at room temperature without needing much heat at all. That is why you can smell a jar of cannabis from across the room. The aroma you smell is terpenes leaving the plant and entering the air. Every hour fresh flower spends warm and exposed is aroma escaping permanently. Freezing halts that loss and preserves the lighter, more fragile compounds that a long dry and cure would drive off.

2

Ice water wash

Agitated in ice water, by hand, with a paddle. Cold snaps the trichome heads off clean — nothing dissolves.

The detail

Cold makes the trichome heads brittle so that gentle agitation snaps them cleanly off the plant. Water is only the transport medium. Resin is not soluble in water, so the heads do not dissolve, they detach and get carried through the system. This is the opposite of a chemical extraction, where a solvent dissolves the target compounds and then has to be evaporated away.

3

Screen separation

The wash runs through stacked mesh screens. Resin heads are caught in the middle range.

The detail

What comes out of the wash vessel is a slurry, meaning solids suspended in liquid rather than dissolved in it. Loose trichome heads, plant fragments, and ice water all moving together. The slurry runs through a stacked series of mesh bags of decreasing size. Larger debris is caught up top, resin heads are captured in the middle range, and fine contaminants pass through the bottom.

4

Freeze dry

Dried under vacuum. The hash is never warm and never wet.

The detail

Wet hash is dried in a freeze dryer under vacuum. Under vacuum, frozen water sublimates, meaning it converts directly from ice into vapor without ever passing through a liquid stage. Skipping the liquid phase matters because the hash is never warm and never wet during drying. Heat and moisture are what degrade terpenes and allow microbial growth.

5

Rosin press

Heated plates, and the resin flows out. The only deliberate heat in the whole process.

The detail

The dried hash is pressed between heated plates. The resin liquefies and flows out. That is the finished rosin. The press is the only point in the process where heat is deliberately applied.

6

Fresh press or cold cure

One material, two finishes: bright and translucent off the press, or cured into an opaque batter.

The detail

Fresh press is jarred straight off the press and stays translucent and sappy. It is the most immediate expression of the material and it is what you want if flavor at its brightest is the goal. Cold cure is held at a controlled low temperature for several days, during which the fats, waxes, and terpene fractions separate and then homogenize into an opaque batter. Cold curing changes texture, rounds the aroma out, and makes the product more stable on the shelf.

Neither finish is better than the other

Fresh press and cold cure are two finishes of the same material. The difference is texture, stability, and how forward the flavor sits. A customer who wants brightness takes fresh press. A customer who wants a rounder profile and something easier to handle takes cold cure. Opacity in a cold cure jar is the cure working, not a defect and not a sign of impurity.

Part One · The Input

Rosin cannot be improved after the fact

A solventless process only concentrates what the plant already produced. The starting material is the entire product.

There is no refining step.

No distillation, no way to strip out what you do not want or add back what you lost.

The detail

This is the structural difference between rosin and most other concentrates. Distillate is refined down until it is close to isolated THC. That process removes the terpenes along with everything else, which leaves a base with no aroma of its own, so terpenes are blended back in afterward. Rosin keeps the original composition intact: the cannabinoids, the full terpene profile, and the flavonoids, in the ratios the plant itself produced.

It also means poor input cannot be hidden. Low grade biomass and trim can be turned into distillate. It cannot be turned into good rosin.

Part One · Cultivation

How we grow

Two environments, both on our own farm. Two sets of strengths — not a ranking.

Environment one

Protected culture

Greenhouse and mixed light. Control, low pest and mold pressure, and a stable environment through the finish.

The detail

The value of growing under protection in solventless is control. A wash cannot correct anything, so what goes in is what comes out. Protected culture means lower pest and mold pressure, no field contamination, and a stable environment through the finish, which produces the cleanest input we have. Every batch of ours that reaches Private Reserve comes from protected culture.

Environment two

Outdoor

Sun grown, full season, full natural spectrum. Broad, complex expression — and the lower impact way to grow.

The detail

Sunlight delivers a complete spectrum at intensities no cultivation light reproduces, and light spectrum is information as much as it is fuel. Different wavelengths trigger different genetic responses. An outdoor plant is exposed continuously to light, wind, temperature swing, soil biology, and pest pressure, and it responds with a broad and complex terpene expression. It is also the lower impact way to grow, since the primary energy input is the sun.

The honest way to frame this at the counter

These are two growing environments with different strengths, not a good one and a bad one. Protected culture gives control and the cleanest input. Outdoor gives full spectrum exposure and complex expression. Our tier ladder reflects how a specific batch graded at the wash bench, not a verdict on either method.

Do not tell a customer one growing method produces a better product than the other. Describe the environments, describe the process, and let them decide.

Part One · Tiers

Tier is assigned to a batch, not to a cut

The single most important thing to understand about tier.

The same cut can grade differently from one run to the next, because every run is different.

Tier is not a THC ranking and it is not a terpene ranking. A Tier 2 jar can test higher than a Private Reserve jar on either number, and that is normal.

How grading works

Grading happens at the wash bench and is based on what the resin actually does: how completely it melts, its color, and the condition of the trichome heads.

Two separate systems. Source lanes: First Water is a single cultivar grown on our own farm; Base Camp is partner farm single cultivars. Material from either lane becomes a batch, a single run of material. Every batch goes through bench grading on melt, color, and head condition, which assigns one of three badges: Private Reserve, Tier 1, or Tier 2. Blends split off from the Base Camp lane before grading and carry no tier at all. Private Reserve requires protected culture growing, and both lanes can reach it. Outdoor material tops out at Tier 1. Tier is assigned to the batch, not the cut, and it is not a potency ranking.

Private Reserve

Comes only from protected culture. That is a requirement, not a guarantee.

The detail

Protected culture means greenhouse, mixed light, or indoor. A protected culture batch still has to grade there on melt, color, and head condition. Plenty of protected culture material grades into Tier 1 or Tier 2 instead.

Tier 1

The cleanest selection off an outdoor run, or protected culture material that graded below Private Reserve.

The detail

Selected on color and head condition. Outdoor material can reach Tier 1 and does not go above it.

Tier 2

The same farm, the same wash, the same standards, and the same testing, at an everyday price.

The detail

Tier 2 is not leftover material and it is not a second thought. It is the rung that lets somebody find out what real solventless tastes like without paying a premium first, and it earns its shelf space on its own. Sell it as what it is rather than as what it is not.

Blends

Blends are not tiered. A blend is built to a profile rather than graded against one, so it carries no tier badge.

What we do not discuss

Customers will ask which strain sits behind a cut name. That is not something we share. Sell the cut name and the profile.

Do not describe a flavor unless approved copy exists for that cut. If none exists, describe the line and the process instead. Never improvise a tasting note.

Part One · On the Shelf

Reading a jar

There is a lot of folklore here, and most of it is wrong. What color and clarity actually tell you:

Color is a signal, not a guarantee.

Lighter generally tracks with fresher material, a clean wash, and low plant matter carryover.

The detail

It is a reasonable signal and it is not a guarantee of anything. Color is not cannabinoid content, and darker does not mean contaminated.

Clarity and opacity are texture, not purity.

Fresh press is translucent because it has not been cured. An opaque jar is not a dirty jar.

The detail

Cold cure is opaque because fats, waxes, and terpene fractions have homogenized. That is the cure working.

What actually tells you: melt, nose, and the COA.

How it melts, how it smells when the jar opens, and the certificate of analysis. Those three, in that order, are what an experienced buyer is reading.

Part One · Vocabulary

Terms customers will use

Know these so you are not caught out. Do not promise a format we are not currently selling.

Hash of the highest melt grade, vaporizing almost completely with little residue. A grade of hash, not a type of rosin.
Live rosin
Rosin pressed from hash that was made from fresh frozen material. Live refers to the fresh frozen input, not to the press.

Part Two · Solventless Education

The background that makes the product make sense

Rosin is a niche category and most customers meeting it for the first time have no frame of reference.

What solventless actually means: only ice, water, heat, and pressure.

No butane, propane, ethanol, or CO2 touches the material at any point. Nothing is added and nothing is distilled out.

The detail

What ends up in the jar is the resin gland itself, the trichome head that grew on the plant, separated mechanically and pressed into oil.

Because no solvent is ever introduced, there is nothing to purge and nothing that can remain behind. Solvent based concentrates are tested for residual solvent because trace amounts can survive into the finished oil. That test category does not apply to a product where no solvent was ever used.

Trichomes: the chemistry lives in the trichome, not the plant tissue.

That is why washing works at all. You are removing the parts of the plant that hold the chemistry and leaving the rest behind.

The detail
  • The trichome head is structured a little like a grape. There is an outer membrane, and the cannabinoids and terpenes are on the inside.
  • The heads that become rosin are roughly 50 to 100 microns across, about the width of a human hair. Everything a customer is paying for sits in structures they can barely see.
  • Trichomes are the plant’s own sunscreen. Resin absorbs ultraviolet radiation before it reaches the tissue underneath, which is the leading explanation for why the plant concentrates these compounds on the surfaces most exposed to light.

Terpenes: everything we value about the smell of cannabis is a plant defense mechanism.

It predates our interest in it by thousands of years.

The detail
  • Terpenes are secondary metabolites. A primary metabolite is something a plant makes to live day to day, like sugars and proteins. A secondary metabolite is something it makes to defend itself against UV, insects, fungus, and grazing animals.
  • Plants evolved terpene production long before humans cultivated anything, to repel insects, deter fungal and bacterial attack, and in some cases attract the predators of their pests.
  • Terpenes are not unique to cannabis. Limonene is what makes citrus peel smell like citrus, pinene is what makes a pine forest smell like pine, and linalool is the main aromatic compound in lavender. Cannabis just produces an unusually complex mix.
  • Cannabis produces well over a hundred cannabinoids and hundreds of aromatic compounds. A distillate isolates one of them. Rosin keeps the profile intact.

THCA and THC: heat converts THCA into THC. That is what decarboxylation means.

Smoking or dabbing decarboxylates in real time, because the material is being heated as it is consumed.

The detail

THCA is the acid form the plant actually produces. It is not decarboxylated, so it does not cross the blood brain barrier the way THC does. This is why a COA lists both THCA and Total THC, and why Total THC is the number that reflects what the consumer experiences.

The entourage effect: state it as a theory, because that is what it is.

The working theory that the character of a cultivar comes from the whole mixture rather than from THC alone.

The detail

The entourage effect is the working theory in cannabis science that cannabinoids, terpenes, and flavonoids interact with one another rather than acting in isolation. It remains an area of active research rather than settled science.

It is the reason experienced customers describe rosin as presenting like the specific plant it came from, while stripped and reconstituted products tend to feel interchangeable regardless of the name on the package.

Part Two · Lab Results

Reading a certificate of analysis

Every jar has a batch specific COA behind it. Numbers change with every run, so read the COA for the lot actually on the shelf rather than quoting a number from memory or from a sales sheet.

Total terpenes is the number worth showing.

Most competing menu tags display THC and nothing else. Terpenes are where rosin distinguishes itself.

The detail

Many point of sale systems have the terpene field and leave it blank. Ask your manager to populate it.

A lower THC number is not a downgrade.

Aged material loses potency slowly while the expression develops. Rosin customers are buying consistency, aroma, and terpene content.

Never quote a number you have not confirmed against the COA for that specific lot.

Our published certificates are at adirondackcoldwater.com/coa, searchable by batch code.

Part Two · Care

Handling, storage, and dab temperature

Store cold.

Rosin is a live, unstable product and refrigeration preserves the volatile compounds.

The detail

Those volatile compounds are the terpenes, and they are the entire reason to buy rosin over something cheaper. Once they evaporate they are gone and cannot be replaced. That is exactly why distillate is stable at room temperature: there is nothing volatile left in it to lose.

Run it low: roughly 450 to 500°F.

Suggest a lower dab temperature than a customer may be used to, to preserve flavor.

The detail

Running too hot burns the terpenes off before they are ever tasted.

Use a clean tool and close the jar.

Air and warmth are what cost the customer the thing they paid for.

Part Three · At the Counter

Talking points

Lead with the process, not with superlatives. These are written to be said as written.

This is made with ice water, heat, and pressure. No chemical solvents at any stage. There is nothing added and nothing to purge out.
Most oil on the shelf is distilled down toward pure THC, which takes the flavor out with everything else, so terpenes get blended back in afterward. Rosin never had anything taken out, so what you taste is the actual plant. There is a theory called the entourage effect, that the cannabinoids and terpenes work together in the ratios the plant grew them in. Pull them apart and rebuild it and you do not get the same thing back.
Rosin cannot be fixed after the fact, so it can only be as good as the flower that went into it. This came off the farm that made it.
If you like flower but want something stronger, this is the closest step up you can take without changing what you are actually consuming. Same plant, same profile, same flavor, just the resin separated out and concentrated.
It is grown, washed, pressed, and jarred by one small farm in Granville, New York. Every step happens under one roof.

If a customer asks

What is the difference between First Water and Base Camp?

First Water is single cultivar from the farm that makes it. Base Camp is partner farm material and all of the blends. Same process and same standards, different source. It is not a ranking.

What does Private Reserve mean?

It is a grade assigned to that specific batch based on how the resin performs at the bench, and it only comes from protected culture growing. It is not a THC ranking.

Why is the THC lower than the jar next to it?

Some of our material is aged, and potency comes down slowly while the expression develops. Rosin buyers are buying consistency, aroma, and terpenes. Compare the terpene numbers, and notice how few products next to it publish one.

Why is this one cloudy and that one clear?

That is the finish, not the quality. Clear and sappy is fresh press, jarred straight off the press. Cloudy is cold cure, held cold for several days until it homogenizes into a batter. Different texture, different aroma character, same material.

What strain is it really?

That is not something the farm shares. The cut name is the name.

Is this better than the solvent concentrates?

Describe the difference rather than ranking them. It is made without solvents and without added terpenes, so nothing is purged out and nothing is blended in. Let the customer decide what matters to them.

Part Three · Compliance

What not to say

  • No health, medical, therapeutic, or curative claims of any kind. That includes anxiety, sleep, pain, and wellness.
  • Do not say rosin is healthier or better for you. Describe the process and let the customer draw the conclusion.
  • No comparative potency or quality claims that are not supported by the COA for that batch.
  • Do not claim or imply that any cannabis product is approved, endorsed, or certified by a government body.
  • Do not say the product is grown or crafted in the Adirondacks. Granville, New York is the accurate claim.
  • Do not claim first, only, best, or finest about anything.
  • Do not say which cultivar sits behind a cut name.
  • Do not describe a flavor for a cut without approved copy.
  • Do not quote a lab number without checking the COA for that lot.
  • Do not name or promise a product that has not launched.
New York compliance, in one line

Under 9 NYCRR Part 128, a claim made at the counter is regulated the same as a claim printed on the package. Stick to what the COA and the label state: cannabinoid content, terpene content, batch and license information, and testing status.
